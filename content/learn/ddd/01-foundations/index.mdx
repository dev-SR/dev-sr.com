## Problem with an Anemic Domain Model

An anemic domain model occurs when domain objects contain mostly data and little or no behavior. Business rules are placed outside the model, usually in services or controllers.

This leads to several problems:

- Entities become passive data containers.
- Business logic is separated from the data it acts on.
- Objects do not protect their own validity.
- Invalid states can be created easily.
- Invariants are enforced externally, if they are enforced at all.
- Business rules become scattered across services.
- Duplication increases as the system grows.
- The code becomes harder to maintain and evolve.
- Services become bloated with responsibilities.
- The domain language is weak or missing.
- Testing becomes harder because behavior is not naturally located in the model.

The main issue is that the model does not represent the business. It only stores data.

---

## Need for Domain-Driven Design

Domain-Driven Design is needed when the business logic is complex enough that it must be explicitly modeled.

DDD helps by:

- Placing behavior together with the data it belongs to.
- Making business rules explicit in the domain model.
- Protecting invariants inside entities, value objects, and aggregates.
- Reducing scattered and duplicated logic.
- Improving communication between technical and business teams.
- Creating a model that reflects real business concepts.
- Making complex systems easier to understand, test, and change.
- Keeping application services focused on coordination instead of business rules.

In short, DDD is needed when the domain is too important to be hidden inside procedural code. The model should express the business, enforce its rules, and remain maintainable as complexity grows.
